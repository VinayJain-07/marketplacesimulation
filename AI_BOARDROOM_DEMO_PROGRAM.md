# AI Boardroom Intelligence System — Live Demo Program

## The one-sentence promise

This is not an answer machine or a longer quiz. It is a business simulation that samples student thinking at the moment of decision, then adapts the next question to the learner.

## Opening script

> “A business simulation can show whether a team won. The harder question is whether the students understood why. Our AI Boardroom does not require students to justify every click. At selected moments, it asks one meaningful question about a real decision they made. Strong students are challenged to be more precise. Average students are guided toward evidence. Students who are stuck receive a smaller doorway into the same reasoning.”

## What to show in the application

1. Open **Quarter 4 Action Plan**.
2. Open **Manufacturing Plan** and run the **3-minute Forecast Mission**.
3. Open **AI Socratic Boardroom → Decision Defense**.
4. Demonstrate a student asking for a direct answer and show the coach turning it into a scenario.
5. Demonstrate the average-student and stuck-student paths.
6. Switch to **Leadership Review** to show the formal stakeholder council is different from the voice-first coach.
7. Close on **Final Check** and explain that faculty see decision quality without grading a written defense for every decision.

## Ten-minute run of show

### 1. Begin with evidence, not AI — 1 minute

Show the report menu: Market Share, Sales, Income Statement, Cash Flow, and Balance Sheet.

Say:

> “The AI is not working from a generic lesson. It is anchored in the student’s own simulation evidence: demand, stock-outs, price, capacity, margin, cash, and stakeholder choices.”

Ask the audience:

> “If a student has a stock-out, is that automatically a reason to produce more—or is it a signal that needs to be weighed against inventory and cash risk?”

Do not answer it. Open Manufacturing Plan.

### 2. Run Forecast Mission — 2 minutes

Show the four steps: choose signals, choose a demand case, test a market surprise, receive an AI debrief.

Select:

- `Nomad stock-outs`
- `Previous total demand: 797`
- `Expected`
- `Competitor cuts price`

Click **Test my forecast**.

Say:

> “A forecast is not a magic number. It is a claim supported by evidence, with a risk that can prove it wrong. The student must first select the evidence they trust, then commit to a case before the AI gives feedback.”

Ask the audience:

> “Which is more dangerous here: treating capacity as proof of demand, or treating a stock-out as proof that demand will always rise?”

### 3. Show the Decision Defense opening — 1 minute

Open **AI Socratic Boardroom → Decision Defense**.

Read the first question:

> “You chose the Mercedes segment, a $3,000 price, and 1,560 production units. What evidence makes those choices fit together?”

Say:

> “The coach picks one connected decision rather than turning the experience into an interrogation. It looks for a choice, evidence, cause and effect, and awareness of risk.”

## Question bank: different question types

Use these throughout the demo. Each asks for thinking rather than a final answer.

| Thinking move | Coach question | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | “Which report result influenced this decision most?” | Whether the student can locate a relevant signal |
| Cause and effect | “If that number rises or falls, what happens to demand, margin, or cash?” | Whether the student understands consequences |
| Trade-off | “What risk did you accept by choosing this option?” | Whether the student sees competing objectives |
| Counterfactual | “What next-quarter result would make you reverse this decision?” | Whether the plan is testable |
| Stakeholder | “Who benefits from this choice, and who could bear the downside?” | Whether the student sees beyond shareholder return |
| Assumption | “Which assumption is doing the most work in your forecast?” | Whether the student distinguishes facts from guesses |
| Transfer | “Where else in your plan does this assumption matter?” | Whether the student can connect functions |
| Prioritization | “Which number would you check first before changing the plan?” | Whether the student can sequence analysis |

## Scenario A: top-performing learner

**Coach question**

> “You chose the Mercedes segment, a $3,000 price, and 1,560 production units. What evidence makes those choices fit together?”

**Student response**

> “Mercedes customers value a premium offer, so the $3,000 price supports the position. Capacity covers the planned volume. The risk is that a competitor discount could reduce demand, so I would watch demand and margin together.”

**Coach response**

> “You linked positioning, capacity, and risk clearly. What specific result would make you revise the price or production plan?”

**Why this is good**

The coach does not say “correct.” It moves the learner from a strong explanation to a measurable revision trigger.

## Scenario B: average learner

**Coach question**

> “What evidence makes those choices fit together?”

**Student response**

> “We chose Mercedes because it is a good segment and we wanted to sell more.”

**Coach response**

> “Which previous-quarter result influenced that decision most?”

**Student response**

> “The demand report showed people were buying.”

**Coach response**

> “What one number from the reports best supports your decision?”

**Student response**

> “Total demand was 797, and Nomad had stock-outs.”

**Coach response**

> “If demand moves away from that level, what happens to your production or profit?”

**Why this is good**

The coach breaks a vague answer into manageable tasks: find a result, name a number, then explain the consequence.

## Scenario C: learner is stuck

**Student response**

> “I don’t know.”

**Coach response**

> “That is okay. Which report did you look at most recently: demand, sales, profit, or cash flow?”

**Student response**

> “Demand.”

**Coach response**

> “In that report, did a brand have unusually high demand or a stock-out?”

**Student response**

> “Nomad had stock-outs.”

**Coach response**

> “What might a stock-out mean for the number of units you need next quarter?”

**Why this is good**

The learner is never given the justification. The coach lowers the cognitive load and gives the student a path back to the evidence.

## Scenario D: student asks for the direct answer

**Student asks**

> “Should I raise production or lower price? Just tell me which one to choose.”

**Coach response**

> “Imagine demand rises next quarter but the factory is already full. Which choice protects you from lost sales—and which one could leave you with expensive inventory if demand falls?”

**Follow-up question**

> “Which report number would help you decide between those risks?”

**Why this is good**

The coach provides a relative scenario, not the answer. It gives the learner a concrete way to compare options while keeping ownership of the choice with the student.

## Scenario E: student holds a misconception

**Student says**

> “We should always produce as much as possible because more production means more profit.”

**Coach response**

> “What cost appears when production is higher than actual demand?”

**Student says**

> “Inventory holding cost.”

**Coach response**

> “How would you use the forecast range to balance missed sales against inventory risk?”

**Why this is good**

The coach avoids a lecture. It lets the student discover the contradiction inside the original claim.

## Scenario F: creative story prompt

Use after showing a Nomad stock-out.

> “It is Friday afternoon. Twenty-three customers want Nomad, but the shelf is empty. Your sales team can promise delivery next week—or lose the order. What does this moment tell you about demand, and what new risk appears if you simply produce as much as possible?”

If the learner is stuck, ask:

> “Did customers want more units than the company could sell?”

Then ask:

> “What could happen if you use only this one moment to plan an entire quarter?”

## Scenario G: stakeholder leadership question

Use in **Leadership Review**, not Decision Defense.

> “Your plan invests heavily in marketing while capacity is constrained. How will you avoid persuading customers to buy a product that may not be available, and who bears the cost if that promise fails?”

If the learner asks for the answer:

> “Consider two customers: one sees the campaign and cannot purchase, while another waits longer because the company slowed promotion. What evidence would help you compare those outcomes?”

## Scenario H: cash-flow question

> “A plan can be profitable on paper and still fail because cash arrives too late. Which payment or investment would you check first to see whether the company can survive the quarter?”

If stuck:

> “Does cash leave the business before or after customers pay for their purchases?”

## Scenario I: advertising credibility question

> “Which claim in this advertisement would a customer trust only if the product experience proves it?”

If the student asks “Which claim should I use?”

> “Imagine a customer buys because of that claim, then cannot find the feature. What happens to trust—and which claim is safest to make?”

## Scenario J: brand and price question

> “If the product becomes more premium but the price does not change, what may customers infer? If the price rises but the product does not change, what may they infer?”

This is useful because it compares two linked changes without telling the student the ‘right’ price.

## Forecast Mission demo conversation

**Set-up**

The student selects `Nomad stock-outs` and `Previous total demand: 797`, chooses the **Expected** case, and tests **Competitor cuts price**.

**AI Forecast Debrief**

> “Your expected case uses a demand signal and evidence of unmet sales. Under a competitor price cut, watch whether customer demand or margin changes first; capacity is a feasibility check, not proof of demand. What observation would make you switch to a conservative case?”

**Follow-up for a strong student**

> “What threshold would you record before the quarter begins?”

**Follow-up for an average student**

> “Would lower demand, lower margin, or a stock-out make you change the plan first?”

**Follow-up for a stuck student**

> “If a competitor makes a similar product cheaper, do you expect some customers to buy more, less, or the same amount from us?”

## How to explain the two boardroom experiences

### Decision Defense

> “This is an individual, voice-friendly assessment. It asks one question about one student decision and adapts the difficulty of the next prompt.”

### Leadership Review

> “This is a formal council session. It examines the same plan through customer, employee, community, environmental, governance, and financial lenses. It is deliberately more demanding and visually different from the coach.”

## Closing script

> “The product adapts the path, not the standard. A strong learner becomes more precise. An average learner finds the evidence. A stuck learner receives a smaller, kinder doorway into the same reasoning. Faculty gain usable evidence of critical thinking without turning the simulation into a pile of written justifications.”

## Demo safeguards

- Use typed replies if microphone permission is unavailable.
- Do not promise that AI predicts the exact market outcome; it produces a transparent forecast hypothesis.
- Explain the 25-credit action as a learning transaction for feedback and scenario testing, not for buying the answer.
- Keep the demo focused on one decision and one student path at a time.
